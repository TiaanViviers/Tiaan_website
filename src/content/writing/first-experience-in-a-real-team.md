This journey started when a good friend recommended the StraTech Internship Programme to me. He had completed it the year before and now works there full time.

I applied for the 2026 programme, landed an interview and, shortly afterwards, met the other five students who would be completing the internship alongside me. It quickly became clear that I would be working with a remarkably capable group of people. The product we delivered at the end of the 3 weeks proved this concretely.

# My Expectation Before the program
Since this was my first internship, I was not entirely sure what to expect.

I assumed we would spend most of our time keeping ourselves busy with a somewhat artificial project. It might not be particularly meaningful, but it would at least expose us to professional software teams, unfamiliar tools and enterprise-scale problems.

The reality looked very different.

A typical day included:
- a daily “Meet the Team” session;
- work on a real proof of concept for a potential product;
- real-world data;
- industry-standard tools, workflows and team practices;
- and a daily Mastery session, which was later replaced by stand-ups and retrospectives as the project intensified.

I was surprised by how much access we were given and how willing people were to share what they knew.

We usually began the day with what became my favourite part of the programme: the Meet the Team sessions. We spoke to people from across the company, including the founders, CEO, CTO, Head of Development, Head of Data Engineering, Head of Product and many others.

They would usually begin with a short presentation, explain some of their background and responsibilities, and then leave plenty of time for questions. These sessions gave us a much broader view of how a software company works than we could have gained by sitting in a dark room and writing code for three weeks.

The middle of the day was mostly reserved for our programme project, Project Beacon.
If you think university moves quickly, the pace of a startup, or a company that has recently moved beyond that stage, is something else entirely. Our team lead and mentors seemed like true masters of their craft, at least from where we were standing. Despite the pace, they created enough structure for us to work effectively while still learning as we went.

What caught most of us off guard was how much time we spent deciding what should be built, why it mattered and how we would work together. At times, it felt as though we spent almost as much time planning the work as implementing it. Before the internship, I had tended to think of planning as something that happened before the real work began. During the programme, I started to understand that the real work starts with planning.

During the first half of the programme, we also attended technical Mastery sessions. These covered the kinds of topics most of us wish received more attention at university, including UI and UX, agentic engineering, SQL, product design and several others.

As Project Beacon gathered momentum, the Mastery sessions made way for stand-ups and retrospectives. That transition reflected the programme well: we were not only being exposed to technical concepts, but also learning how to communicate, organise ourselves and function as useful members of a team.

That balance between developing strong technical individuals and strong teammates was what made the programme so valuable.

# The Shift Left

To understand one of the biggest ideas I took away from the internship, it helps to first think about the software development lifecycle.

The SDLC describes the broad process through which software moves: from identifying a problem and gathering requirements, through design and implementation, to testing, deployment and maintenance. These stages are often presented as separate parts of a neat sequence, each owned by a different role. Product defines the problem, developers build the solution, quality assurance tests it, security reviews it, and operations eventually keeps it running.

In reality the boundaries are almost never that clean. Modern AI is now making them even less distinct.

Large language models, along with the tools and development environments built around them, have improved remarkably quickly. It is easy to forget how recently GPT-3 first became publicly available and how limited those early systems now appear compared with current tools. There are still valid disagreements about where these models are effective, where they create risk, and how much they should be trusted. What seems increasingly difficult to argue, however, is that they can simply be ignored.

For many businesses, AI is becoming a tool that is too expensive not to use. The cost is not only the price of a subscription or infrastructure. It is also the productivity lost when competitors use these tools to explore ideas, produce first drafts, automate repetitive work and move from a problem to a working prototype faster. AI will not replace every part of software development, but it seems likely that it will eventually play some role in the creation of almost every substantial software product.

This changes where human value sits within the development lifecycle.
As implementation becomes faster and cheaper, more of a developer’s value moves towards deciding what should be implemented, how it should work and whether it solves the right problem in the first place. The same applies to nearly every other role involved in building software.

A developer may spend less time producing routine implementation code and more time thinking about architecture, trade-offs and how a feature affects the wider product. A data scientist can build more experiments to test a greater variety of hypothesis, Product teams can produce documentation faster, security teams can automate parts of their analysis, and quality assurance teams can generate and execute more tests with less manual effort.

The result is not that everyone suddenly has less work to do. It is that more people are able (and increasingly expected) to think earlier in the problem cycle.

Instead of only asking how an algorithm should be implemented, a developer may begin asking whether the feature gives the product a meaningful advantage, how it changes the user experience, whether it fits the pricing model, or whether the complexity it introduces is justified. Technical decisions become harder to separate from product and business decisions because the cost of producing the first implementation is no longer always the largest constraint.

This is what I have come to think of as a shift left. The phrase is already used in software engineering to describe activities such as testing or security moving earlier in the development process. I think something broader is now happening. Roles across the lifecycle are being pulled closer to the beginning of the problem.

That shift may also change what software teams look like.

My personal suspicion is that smaller, more cross-functional teams will become increasingly common. One team may be responsible for exploring a problem, speaking to users and rapidly prototyping possible solutions across the full stack. Another may take a promising prototype and turn it into a secure, reliable production system. A smaller long-term team may then maintain, observe and improve it once it is in use.

These boundaries will probably remain fluid, but the people inside them may need to operate across a much wider portion of the lifecycle. Developers will need some product judgment. Product people will need a stronger understanding of technical constraints. Quality, security and operations will need to be considered from the beginning rather than added near the end.

This does not mean that specialists disappear. Deep expertise and understanding of the system as a whole will become increasingly valuable. Technical professionals will increasingly need to understand how their work connects to the whole product rather than only the part immediately in front of them.

One of the things I appreciated most about the internship was being exposed to a company willing to question whether established ways of working could be improved with modern tools. We were not encouraged to use AI simply because it was fashionable, nor to treat existing processes as fixed. We were shown that tools, roles and workflows should all be reconsidered when the environment changes.

For someone entering the industry at a time when those changes are happening quickly, that exposure felt like a privilege.

# The Most Technical People Spoke Mainly About People

One of the things that surprised me most during the internship was what the most senior technical people chose to speak about.

I expected conversations with people like the CTO, Head of Development and senior engineers to focus mainly on architecture, tooling, engineering practices and technical depth. Those topics certainly came up, but they were rarely the main point. Again and again, the conversation returned to communication, trust, responsibility, self-awareness, personal growth and emotional maturity. At first I found this slightly confusing and a bit frustrating.

These were people who had spent years becoming exceptionally good at technical work. I assumed that the further someone progressed in their career, the more specialised and technical their thinking would become. Instead, the people with the most technical experience seemed to be the most convinced that technical ability alone was not enough.

I initially wondered whether the technical work had simply become boring to them. Perhaps they had solved enough difficult problems that the code was no longer the interesting part. By the end of the programme, I thought the opposite was probably closer to the truth. Their experience had shown them how much technical work depends on everything around it.

This was a difficult idea for me to fully appreciate before seeing it in practice. Technical skill is visible and relatively easy to measure. You observe a working system, a well-designed architecture or a difficult problem that someone solved. The value of trust, communication and emotional maturity is less obvious.

A brilliant technical solution has limited value if nobody understands it, trusts it or can work effectively with the person who created it. In the same way, a technically gifted person can become a bottleneck if they cannot communicate clearly, accept criticism or allow others to take ownership.
This was a hard lesson to learn.

I still find some of this slightly counterintuitive. Part of me naturally wants to believe that becoming better means learning more tools, writing better code and building better models. I still believe that those things matter, but the internship showed me that they are only one part of becoming a professional.
