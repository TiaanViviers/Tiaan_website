## Overview

This project was developed during an internship as part of a multidisciplinary team working with anonymised loyalty and transaction data from a panel of franchise restaurants.

We designed a system for franchise restaurants who are already operating in the customer loyalty domain. This product will help a restaurant manager move from passive reporting to forward-looking action.

When a location was forecast to underperform relative to its own historical expectations, the system would:

1. raise a signal;
2. explain the likely drivers;
3. choose a campaign strategy and audience;
4. simulate the projected outcome;
5. present a frozen recommendation for approval or rejection.

The final campaign decision remained human-gated. The objective was not to automate marketing blindly, but to give decision-makers a clearer, evidence-backed starting point.

## The product problem

Traditionally campaigns or vouchers would be generated by looking at retrospective performance of the business. We wanted to see if it is viable to run campaigns as a preventative action to prevent restaurants from experiencing a revenue dip.

The challenge was to connect several different questions:

- Which locations are likely to disappoint relative to expectation?
- Is the problem driven by traffic, spend per visit or customer behaviour?
- Which audience should receive an offer?
- What is the likely economic outcome before anything is sent?
- How should the recommendation be presented so that a human can trust and review it?

This made the project more than a forecasting exercise. Forecasting was only the first stage in a broader decision system.

## Constraints

Several constraints shaped the design:

- Updates and recommendations had to remain usable for a weekly review cycle, not only for offline analysis.
- Only marketing-consent customers could enter campaign audiences.
- The dataset was anonymised, but still treated as confidential operational data.
- The offer shape remained fixed, a set voucher value and validity period.
- The only lever available to us was audience selection.
- Final launch authority stayed with a human reviewer.

## Approach

The modelling pipeline was organised around four main stages.

### Forecast

Revenue was modelled through a decomposition:

$$
\text{revenue}
=
\text{bill count}
\times
\text{average bill value}
$$

Separate LightGBM models estimated customer traffic and average transaction value.

This was useful for more than prediction accuracy. It also made the system easier to diagnose: an underperforming forecast could be linked more naturally to weak visit volume, reduced basket size or a combination of both.

The feature pipeline combined historical sales behaviour with lagged, rolling and calendar variables, including South African holidays and school terms. Evaluation followed a walk-forward design so that each forecast used only information that would have been available at the time.

### Signal

A location was not flagged merely because revenue was expected to fall.

Instead, its forecast was compared with that location’s own historical out-of-sample forecast distribution. Month-conditioned history was preferred when enough prior weeks existed; otherwise the system fell back to a broader store-level baseline.

This allowed the system to identify cases where performance might still be positive in absolute terms, but materially weaker than what would normally be expected for that location and time of year.
Signals were scored with a gap relative to expectation, calibrated into severity bands and persisted as immutable records.

### Context

Raw model features and SHAP values are not useful to most business users.

The context layer re-ran attributions over the forecast window, kept the features that contributed most to downside pressure, and grouped them into broader business concepts such as weekday footfall or spend per visit. Those concepts were then translated into a concise headline, supporting drivers and a problem type.

A separate decomposition asked whether the dip was driven more by traffic, basket size or a mixture of both. That routing decision mattered: the problem type determined which strategy pool the decision system was allowed to search.

Not every signal was treated as decision-ready. Context also decided whether a signal was eligible to proceed into audience selection at all.

### Decide

The offer itself remained fixed: a voucher with predetermined face value, validity window and communication cost. The main decision was **who should receive it**.

Each strategy arm represented a different audience-selection recipe. Some emphasised recently lapsed high-value customers; others focused on weekday regulars or broader win-back segments. Filters defined the segment transparently. Ranking decided who inside that segment was prioritised, first with static rules and later with a learned score once enough simulation labels existed.

Thompson sampling compared arms using simulated net value per issued offer. Early iterations forced exploration so that cold-start strategies were not ignored. The system could run several campaign trials for one signal, receive redemption and spend outcomes from an external simulation service, update its beliefs and freeze the strongest trial for review.

Two learning loops ran in parallel:

- within a signal, the system explored strategies until projected net value stabilised or an iteration budget was reached;
- across signals, strategy beliefs and audience ranking improved as more simulation outcomes accumulated.

The simplified economic objective was:

$$
\text{net value}
=
\text{redeemer spend}
-
\text{voucher cost}
-
\text{communication cost}
$$

This kept optimisation focused on audience quality rather than continuously changing the offer itself.

## Experiments and rejected alternatives

A few design choices were deliberately rejected.

Absolute revenue decline as the only fire rule was too blunt. It missed locations that were still growing, but underperforming relative to seasonal expectation.

A single revenue model was simpler, but weaker for diagnosis. Decomposing traffic and basket size made explanations and strategy routing more useful.

Fully autonomous launching was rejected early. Brand managers needed agency over what went out, even when the recommendation was strong.

Changing voucher value on every trial would have expanded the search space, but also blurred what the system was learning. Keeping the offer fixed concentrated learning on audience selection.

## My role

This was a team project, and I do not claim sole ownership of the full product.

My main contribution sat across the model and decision pipeline: turning anonymised loyalty data into features, forecasts, signals, explanations and simulated campaign recommendations that the review product could consume.

In practice that meant work on:

- the loyalty-data feature pipeline and database load path;
- leakage-safe walk-forward forecasting and model artifact generation;
- signal calibration, severity bands and persistence;
- SHAP-based context, concept translation and problem-type routing;
- strategy definitions, audience recipes and bandit experimentation;
- testing tiers, CI coverage and API security contracts;
- product documentation for parts of the campaign-review experience;
- later additions such as server-side campaign narratives, with fallbacks when generation was unavailable.

I collaborated with teammates across product design, frontend, backend, data infrastructure and deployment.

The delivery process mattered as much as the modelling. Work moved through tickets, pull requests, reviews, protected branches and CI checks. Scope lived in product documents and API contracts, not only in notebooks.

## Product architecture

The proof of concept connected the modelling pipeline to a review product.

Operationally, anonymised loyalty and transaction data lived in PostgreSQL. Feature and forecasting jobs produced the inputs for weekly signal detection. Context and Decide stages attached explanations, strategies and simulated economics. A FastAPI backend exposed campaign state to a React review dashboard, with cloud deployment through AWS and Cloudflare.

The product surface had two main screens:

- a campaign queue for scanning newly recommended locations by severity, projected uplift and underperformance;
- a campaign review view for inspecting rationale, projected economics, audience framing and the approve or reject decision.

Only converged recommendations appeared in the queue. Intermediate exploration trials remained internal to the decision loop.

Browser clients talked only to the API. Database credentials, model artifacts and external-service keys stayed server-side. Approval updated review state and audit history; it did not itself become a live customer send in the delivered scope.

## Outcome

The team delivered an end-to-end proof of concept in which anonymised operational data could move through the full chain:

> forecast → signal → explanation → strategy → simulation → human review

The result included:

- walk-forward forecasting across the restaurant panel;
- calibrated underperformance signals;
- explainable context derived from model features;
- persistent bandit state and simulated campaign trials;
- a campaign queue and detailed review screen;
- API, database and cloud deployment infrastructure;
- CI checks and documented product contracts.

Just as important, the team left behind the operating discipline around the code: documented interfaces, protected promotion into deployable branches, and an explicit boundary between simulated optimisation and real customer communication.

The project stopped short of becoming a fully autonomous campaign platform. Scheduling, real campaign dispatch and parts of the post-campaign learning interface remained outside the delivered scope.

## Limitations

The system remained a proof of concept.

Campaign generation was batch-driven rather than fully scheduled, approval did not trigger real customer communication, and the simulation service acted as a black box for campaign response.

The quality of the recommendations also depended on forecast calibration, signal thresholds and the assumptions encoded in the available audience strategies.
.

## Lessons learned

This project reinforced that production machine learning is mostly about the connections between components.

The forecasting model mattered, but so did:

- leakage-safe evaluation;
- stable database contracts;
- translating model output into language a user can understand;
- preserving immutable campaign snapshots;
- separating simulation from real action;
- keeping secrets and model artifacts away from the browser;
- allowing a human to reject the model’s recommendation;
- keeping configuration for demos separate from default detection behaviour;
- writing product contracts before treating a screen as finished.

It also showed me how much stronger technical work becomes inside a team where product, engineering and modelling decisions are reviewed together.
